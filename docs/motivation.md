# Motivation: why the distribution of the target was suspected, and what the data say

Written while drafting, on 2026-09-20. It is the source for the paper's Introduction and part of its Discussion. Every number here comes from the committed data and metrics of this repository; the commands that produce them are given at the end.

## 1. Where the question came from

The study started from practice, about two years before this note. The aim was a cheap, fast surrogate for slope stability. The training data were generated by sampling the parameters uniformly, yet the models were weak. The working explanation at the time was that the **target** was covered unevenly: a uniform design in the parameter space produces few slopes with a low factor of safety, and many comfortably stable ones.

That explanation is plausible and widespread. It is also testable, and this study tests it.

## 2. Why the data must be numerical

A factor of safety cannot be measured. An experiment or a case history yields a binary outcome: the slope stood, or it failed. And it yields it mostly near the limit state, where failures occur. So:

- **Values of FOS exist only as the output of an analysis.** Every training point costs a solver run, which makes the budget of analyses the relevant economic quantity.
- **An experimental programme cannot substitute for it.** Field evidence supports a stable/failed classification, not a regression target. The study is therefore a regression study on simulated data, and no threshold or classification metric is used.

## 3. The cost of an error is asymmetric

Predicting 4 when the truth is 6 changes no decision: both are comfortable. Predicting 1.3 when the truth is 0.8 changes everything. This is the engineering reason for the reporting zones and for the primary criterion:

| zone | meaning |
|---|---|
| FOS < 0.5 | outside the selection range; a slope that cannot stand |
| 0.5–1 | unstable; the starting state of any remediation |
| 1–2 | the decision zone, where geometry, drainage and reinforcement are chosen |
| 2–6 | a comfortable reserve, where the exact value hardly matters |
| ≥ 6 | outside the selection range |

The primary criterion is log-RMSE on the **decision range** [0.5, 2), log-symmetric around 1. Both halves are reported, because they behave differently.

## 4. What the data say about the original hypothesis

### 4.1 Uniform parameter sampling is not, by itself, the problem

With a tuned gradient-boosting model, LHS data give an accurate surrogate:

| arm | training data | log-RMSE, test-natural |
|---|---|---|
| A | seed LHS 300 | 0.088 |
| B | seed + LHS 300 | 0.059 |

Roughly 6% relative error at 600 analyses, on data that are uniform in the parameter space. The weak models of two years ago are therefore more likely to be explained by the learner, its hyper-parameters, the representation and the metric than by the target's distribution:

- the derived feature `gamma_H_c = gamma*H/c`, the stability number, carries most of the response;
- the model is trained on log(FOS), which matches the multiplicative nature of the errors;
- library-default hyper-parameters are not neutral at n = 300: `min_samples_leaf = 20` is a far stronger constraint there than at n = 600;
- RMSE on the FOS scale is dominated by the largest values: on test-natural, 87% of the squared error comes from the 24 points with FOS ≥ 6.

### 4.2 Only part of the low-FOS range is rare

Share of points per zone (the seed, the LHS extension and test-natural are all LHS in the parameter space):

| set | < 0.5 | 0.5–1 | 1–2 | 2–6 | ≥ 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| seed (LHS 300) | 3% | 10% | 34% | 47% | 7% |
| LHS extension | 2% | 11% | 30% | 48% | 8% |
| test-natural | 3% | 11% | 31% | 46% | 8% |
| uniform in log over [0.5, 6] | | 28% | 28% | 44% | |

So under LHS:
- **0.5–1 is rare:** about 10–11% of the points, against the 28% a log-uniform design would place there;
- **1–2 is not rare:** about 30–34%, more than log-uniform would give it.

Uniformizing the response over the whole selection range therefore *reduces* the training density in the decision zone. That is a structural property of this problem, not an accident of one design.

### 4.3 The decision zone is the flattest part of the response

The intuition that relations are more sensitive near the limit state was tested in two independent ways.

**From the solved data, model-free.** For each point, the five nearest neighbours in the normalized parameter space; the median of |Δ log FOS| divided by the distance (2 250 points of every set):

| zone | median \|Δ log FOS\| / distance | median \|Δ FOS\| / distance |
|---|---|---|
| < 0.5 | 1.50 | 0.64 |
| 0.5–1 | 1.04 | 0.82 |
| **1–2** | **0.83** | 1.18 |
| 2–6 | 0.94 | 3.06 |
| ≥ 6 | 1.53 | 9.92 |

**From the tuned GBM of arm B,** central finite differences of log FOS with respect to each parameter, normalized by that parameter's range, at the 300 points of test-natural. The median gradient norm is 4.05 below 0.5, 3.16 in 0.5–1, **2.20 in 1–2**, 2.63 in 2–6 and 8.23 above 6.

Both agree: **1–2 is the flattest region in log space.** The steep regions are the two tails, and for understandable reasons. With FOS ≈ c·N/(γH) + tanφ·M, the relative sensitivity to cohesion behaves like 1/c, which explodes as FOS falls; at high FOS the height is small, and the sensitivity to H explodes.

On the FOS scale, sensitivity grows monotonically with FOS.

So the decision zone 1–2 is neither rare nor steep. It is only **important**.

### 4.4 Where the three conditions do coincide

| criterion | 0.5–1 | 1–2 |
|---|---|---|
| rare under LHS | yes (10–11% against 28%) | no (30–34%) |
| locally steep | yes (1.04) | no (0.83, the flattest) |
| engineering relevance | the starting state of a remediation | the design decisions themselves |
| observed effect of guided allocation | wins in 3 of 4 family × test cells, by 24–41% | loses in 4 of 4 |

This is the study's central observation. Response-space allocation targets **rarity**. Rarity, difficulty and relevance coincide only below FOS 1, and that is exactly where the guided designs gain. In the decision zone they lose, because LHS already covers it more densely than a log-uniform target would and because the response there is flat.

Part of the gain below 1 is simply density: guided-log puts 97 points in 0.5–1, against about 33 for an LHS extension. That is the mechanism, not a confound, but it must be said explicitly.

## 5. The thesis that follows

> At a fixed simulation budget, uniformizing the response space does not improve global accuracy over uniform sampling of the parameter space. It relocates accuracy along the FOS axis, following the training density actually achieved rather than the one intended; the screener controls the gap between the two. The advantage appears only where rarity, difficulty and engineering relevance coincide — in this problem, below FOS 1. In the decision zone 1–2, which LHS already covers well and where the response is flat, guided allocation loses.

Two caveats travel with it:
- one realization per design so far; the replications measure the between-design variability;
- the decision-range criterion was defined after the C-log results and before C-seq was run; it is built only from the lower end of the selection range and its log mirror around 1.

## 6. Reproducing the numbers here

- **Zone shares (4.2):** `data.load_role(...)` and `design.bin_counts`, per zone with `config.ZONE_EDGES`.
- **Neighbour sensitivity (4.3):** `scipy.spatial.cKDTree` on `data.normalize`, k = 6, on the concatenation of seed, lhs-extension, test-natural, guided, guided-log and test-stratified.
- **Gradient sensitivity (4.3):** `models.load_fit("gbm", "B", "log-rmse")`, central differences of ±5% of each parameter's range, `data.add_features` recomputed after each perturbation.
- **Zone errors (4.4):** `outputs/metrics/test-*__log-rmse.json`, `arms[family][arm]["zones"]`.
- **Predictions and their outcomes:** `docs/predictions-and-outcomes.md`.
