# Motivation: why the distribution of the target was suspected, and what the data say

Written while drafting, on 2026-09-20. It is the source for the paper's Introduction and part of its Discussion. Every number here comes from the committed data and metrics of this repository; the commands that produce them are given at the end.

## 0. Title and venue

Decided 2026-09-20, with the replications in hand.

**Title.** *Response-balanced sampling relocates rather than improves accuracy in slope-stability surrogates under a fixed simulation budget.* The verb is the one in the thesis of `CLAUDE.md` and in §6 below; the paper uses no synonym for it. The earlier title, *Surrogate-Guided Sampling for Machine Learning Prediction of Slope Stability*, was dropped because it promises the method that the data refuted.

**Venue.** **Data-Centric Engineering** (Cambridge University Press). The question the study asks — how a fixed budget of expensive analyses should be allocated across the response space — is data-centric by construction rather than an application of ML to slopes, and the venue publishes methodological and negative results. *Scientific Reports* is the fallback: its criterion is soundness rather than novelty, but it offers no disciplinary readership and a substantial APC. *Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence* was set aside — not as too ambitious, but as mismatched: it expects an application that works, and the headline here is that it does not systematically. Mazloom et al., *Eng. Appl. Artif. Intell.* **165** (2026) 113398, sits in that venue and fits the pattern: a method, multimodal distillation, that works on three benchmarks. Read on 2026-09-21, it is about distillation and not about sampling (`docs/related-work.md` §5).

## 1. Where the question came from

The study started from practice, about two years before this note. The aim was a cheap, fast surrogate for slope stability. The training data were generated by sampling the parameters uniformly, yet the models were weak. The working explanation at the time was that the **target** was covered unevenly: a uniform design in the parameter space produces few slopes with a low factor of safety, and many comfortably stable ones.

That explanation is plausible and widespread. It is also testable, and this study tests it.

## 2. Why the data must be numerical

A factor of safety cannot be measured. An experiment or a case history yields a binary outcome: the slope stood, or it failed. And it yields it mostly near the limit state, where failures occur. So:

- **Values of FOS exist only as the output of an analysis.** Every training point costs a solver run, which makes the budget of analyses the relevant economic quantity.
- **An experimental programme cannot substitute for it.** Field evidence supports a stable/failed classification, not a regression target. The study is therefore a regression study on simulated data, and no threshold or classification metric is used.

## 3. The cost of an error is asymmetric

Predicting 4 when the truth is 6 changes no decision: both are comfortable. Predicting 1.3 when the truth is 0.8 changes everything. This is the engineering reason for the reporting zones and for the primary criterion:

| zone | meaning |
|---|---|
| FOS < 0.5 | outside the selection range; a slope that cannot stand |
| 0.5–1 | unstable; the starting state of any remediation |
| 1–2 | the decision zone, where geometry, drainage and reinforcement are chosen |
| 2–6 | a comfortable reserve, where the exact value hardly matters |
| ≥ 6 | outside the selection range |

The primary criterion is log-RMSE on the **decision range** [0.5, 2), log-symmetric around 1. Both halves are reported, because they behave differently.

**Independent corroboration of both limits.** Mazloom et al. (BARL, Applied Soft Computing 188, 2026), working with a different solver (FLAC8), a different constitutive model and a wider parameter set, arrive at the same two numbers without reference to this study. Their training set is filtered to **FOS ∈ [0.3, 6]**, samples outside the interval being discarded (§3.3), and they state their motivation as: "while most practical scenarios involve FoS values between 0.9 and 2, our goal is to construct the model to predict a broader range, extending from 0.3 to 6" (§2.2). Our upper limit of 6 and the upper edge of the decision range at 2 are therefore not idiosyncratic choices. The lower cut differs — 0.3 against our 0.5 — and ours is the one that follows from log-symmetry around 1.

## 4. What the data say about the original hypothesis

### 4.1 Uniform parameter sampling is not, by itself, the problem

With a tuned gradient-boosting model, LHS data give an accurate surrogate:

| arm | training data | log-RMSE, test-natural |
|---|---|---|
| A | seed LHS 300 | 0.088 |
| B | seed + LHS 300 | 0.059 |

Roughly 6% relative error at 600 analyses, on data that are uniform in the parameter space. The weak models of two years ago are therefore more likely to be explained by the learner, its hyper-parameters, the representation and the metric than by the target's distribution:

- the derived feature `gamma_H_c = gamma*H/c`, the stability number, carries most of the response;
- the model is trained on log(FOS), which matches the multiplicative nature of the errors;
- library-default hyper-parameters are not neutral at n = 300: `min_samples_leaf = 20` is a far stronger constraint there than at n = 600;
- RMSE on the FOS scale is dominated by the largest values: on test-natural, 87% of the squared error comes from the 24 points with FOS ≥ 6.

### 4.2 Only part of the low-FOS range is rare

Share of points per zone (the seed, the LHS extension and test-natural are all LHS in the parameter space):

| set | < 0.5 | 0.5–1 | 1–2 | 2–6 | ≥ 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| seed (LHS 300) | 3% | 10% | 34% | 47% | 7% |
| LHS extension | 2% | 11% | 30% | 48% | 8% |
| test-natural | 3% | 11% | 31% | 46% | 8% |
| uniform in log over [0.5, 6] | | 28% | 28% | 44% | |

So under LHS:
- **0.5–1 is rare:** about 10–11% of the points, against the 28% a log-uniform design would place there;
- **1–2 is not rare:** about 30–34%, more than log-uniform would give it.

Uniformizing the response over the whole selection range therefore *reduces* the training density in the decision zone. That is a structural property of this problem, not an accident of one design.

**But it is a property of this parameter domain, not of slope stability.** BARL's LHS set, filtered to the same kind of interval, is skewed the *other* way: 25% of its points lie below FOS 1.29 and the median is 2.09, so the region its authors call under-represented is 4–6, not 0.5–1 (their Table 1 and §5.4). Same family of designs, mirror-image imbalance, because the parameter ranges differ. Whichever direction the skew runs, the argument of this study is the same — the allocation follows the density actually achieved — but no claim may be made about *which* zone is rare in general.

### 4.3 The decision zone is the flattest part of the response

The intuition that relations are more sensitive near the limit state was tested in two independent ways.

**From the solved data, model-free.** For each point, the five nearest neighbours in the normalized parameter space; the median of |Δ log FOS| divided by the distance (2 250 points of every set):

| zone | median \|Δ log FOS\| / distance | median \|Δ FOS\| / distance |
|---|---|---|
| < 0.5 | 1.50 | 0.64 |
| 0.5–1 | 1.04 | 0.82 |
| **1–2** | **0.83** | 1.18 |
| 2–6 | 0.94 | 3.06 |
| ≥ 6 | 1.53 | 9.92 |

**From the tuned GBM of arm B,** central finite differences of log FOS with respect to each parameter, normalized by that parameter's range, at the 300 points of test-natural. The median gradient norm is 4.05 below 0.5, 3.16 in 0.5–1, **2.20 in 1–2**, 2.63 in 2–6 and 8.23 above 6.

Both agree: **1–2 is the flattest region in log space.** The steep regions are the two tails, and for understandable reasons. With FOS ≈ c·N/(γH) + tanφ·M, the relative sensitivity to cohesion behaves like 1/c, which explodes as FOS falls; at high FOS the height is small, and the sensitivity to H explodes.

On the FOS scale, sensitivity grows monotonically with FOS.

So the decision zone 1–2 is neither rare nor steep. It is only **important**.

### 4.4 Where the three conditions do coincide

| criterion | 0.5–1 | 1–2 |
|---|---|---|
| rare under LHS | yes (10–11% against 28%) | no (30–34%) |
| locally steep | yes (1.04) | no (0.83, the flattest) |
| engineering relevance | the starting state of a remediation | the design decisions themselves |
| observed effect of guided allocation | wins in 3 of 4 family × test cells, by 24–41% | loses in 4 of 4 |

This is the study's central observation. Response-space allocation targets **rarity**. Rarity, difficulty and relevance coincide only below FOS 1, and that is exactly where the guided designs gain. In the decision zone they lose, because LHS already covers it more densely than a log-uniform target would and because the response there is flat.

Part of the gain below 1 is simply density: guided-log puts 97 points in 0.5–1, against about 33 for an LHS extension. That is the mechanism, not a confound, but it must be said explicitly.

## 5. What this study does not do: filter the training set on the response

The question is prompted by BARL, which builds its training set by running the solver on an LHS design and then **discarding every analysis whose FOS falls outside [0.3, 6]**, keeping 300. It is worth stating plainly what we do instead, and why, because the two choices look similar and are not.

**We never discard a solved point.** The only exclusions in `sgs` are `solver_error` and `unresolved` (`data.EXCLUSION_REASONS`); no set is filtered by the value of FOS. The selection range constrains which candidates the guided arm may *choose* from the pool, using the screener's **predicted** FOS — that is, before the analysis is paid for. Whatever the solver then returns is kept. The difference is not cosmetic:

- BARL's filter is applied to the **true** FOS, so it runs the solver and then throws the result away. At a fixed budget those runs are spent and yield no training point. Ours is applied to a **predicted** FOS, so nothing paid for is wasted.
- A filter on the true value cannot be applied at deployment, because the true value is what the model is for.

**The tails in our guided sets are screener errors, and they are load-bearing.** Because the gate is on a prediction, points still land outside the range: guided-log has 45 points below 0.5 and 19 above 6, guided has 3 and 37, the three sequential batches 32 and 24 together. Those points were never intended, and they are the only reason the guided arms can say anything about the tails.

### 5.1 What truncating the training response costs

A tree ensemble cannot predict outside the range of its training targets. Truncation therefore does not degrade tail accuracy gradually — it caps it. The arms here measure the size of the effect, because the guided sets are truncated near 6 by construction while the LHS sets are not:

| training set | n with FOS ≥ 6 | max FOS | tail quartiles |
|---|---|---|---|
| seed (LHS) | 20 | 21.05 | 7.99 / 10.36 / 12.83 |
| LHS extension | 25 | 22.46 | 7.10 / 8.50 / 11.56 |
| guided | 37 | 11.45 | 6.47 / 6.94 / 8.00 |
| guided-log | 19 | 8.46 | 6.28 / 6.60 / 7.20 |

The guided sets hold **more** points above 6 than the seed and still reach barely past it. Zone FOS ≥ 6 on test-natural, GBM, 24 test points:

| arm | training | log-RMSE | MAE | bias |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | seed only | 0.108 | 0.80 | −0.54 |
| B | seed + LHS | 0.078 | 0.55 | −0.38 |
| C-log | seed + guided-log | 0.095 | 0.75 | −0.47 |
| **C₀-log** | **guided-log only** | **0.508** | **3.16** | **−3.15** |
| C₀ | guided only | 0.485 | 3.20 | −3.20 |
| C₀-seq | sequential only | 0.490 | 3.08 | −3.04 |

In every C₀ arm the bias equals the MAE to two decimals: **every** tail point is under-predicted. That is saturation, not noise. The obvious alternative explanation was checked and fails — the median distance from a tail test point to its nearest training point is 0.249 for C₀ and 0.250 for A, so parameter-space coverage is the same; what differs is that C₀'s training response stops at 11.45 with most of its mass at 6.9, while A's reaches 21.05.

**Consequence for the question.** Filtering on the response is justified only if deployment is conditional on the same filter — that is, only if something independent and cheap establishes that a case lies inside the range before the surrogate is asked. Otherwise the model is deployed on a distribution whose tail it structurally cannot represent, and the failure is invisible in any metric computed on a test set filtered the same way. BARL's training maximum is 5.98 and its test pool is restricted to the same interval, so its reported R² = 0.97 is conditional on the filter. Our test-natural set keeps the full LHS response distribution, tails included, which is what makes the numbers above measurable at all.

This also sharpens the reading of the guided arms: part of what the response-space allocation buys below FOS 1 is paid for above 6, and the seed's unfiltered LHS backbone is what keeps the bill small.

## 6. The thesis that follows

> At a fixed simulation budget, uniformizing the response space does not improve global accuracy over uniform sampling of the parameter space. It relocates accuracy along the FOS axis, following the training density actually achieved rather than the one intended; the screener controls the gap between the two. The advantage appears only where rarity, difficulty and engineering relevance coincide — in this problem, below FOS 1. In the decision zone 1–2, which LHS already covers well and where the response is flat, guided allocation loses.

**What the replications settled** (2026-09-20; five new realizations of the added points, details and intervals in `docs/predictions-and-outcomes.md`). The relocation is the part that holds: across the four family × test cells it has the predicted sign in 0.5–1 and in 1–2 for both guided variants, 16 of 16 by sign, with 10 of the 16 Welch intervals excluding 0. It is a property of the strategy, not of the single draw the variants were built on — on that draw the gain side held in only 2 to 3 of the 4 cells. The cost side, in 1–2, is the better resolved of the two.

Four caveats travel with it:
- on the decision range, the primary criterion, no guided arm's interval against B is entirely below 0 in any of the eight cells, and one cell puts C-log *above* B; an interval containing 0 is inconclusive, never a tie and never evidence of equivalence;
- "the screener controls the gap" is the weakest clause. C-seq retrains the screener after every batch and does not measurably change the outcome, under either family; the advantage it appeared to hold over C-log under RF was an artifact of one draw;
- the intervals cover between-design variability only. Seed variability is not measured at all — the LHS-300 seed is fixed by design — the two test sets are fixed, so their sampling error is common to every realization and never averages out, and the fits use common hyper-parameters fixed from the seed, so the replications do not replicate the tuned comparison;
- the decision-range criterion was defined after the C-log results and before C-seq was run; it is built only from the lower end of the selection range and its log mirror around 1. The paper's title rests on this thesis, so the post-hoc origin of the criterion belongs in Methods, stated plainly.

## 7. Reproducing the numbers here

- **Zone shares (4.2):** `data.load_role(...)` and `design.bin_counts`, per zone with `config.ZONE_EDGES`.
- **Neighbour sensitivity (4.3):** `scipy.spatial.cKDTree` on `data.normalize`, k = 6, on the concatenation of seed, lhs-extension, test-natural, guided, guided-log and test-stratified.
- **Gradient sensitivity (4.3):** `models.load_fit("gbm", "B", "log-rmse")`, central differences of ±5% of each parameter's range, `data.add_features` recomputed after each perturbation.
- **Zone errors (4.4):** `outputs/metrics/test-*__log-rmse.json`, `arms[family][arm]["zones"]`.
- **Training tails and zone bias (5.1):** `data.load_role(...)["FOS"]` per role for the tail table; `outputs/metrics/test-natural__log-rmse.json`, `arms[family][arm]["zones"]`, last entry, for log-RMSE, MAE and bias; `scipy.spatial.cKDTree` on `data.normalize` for the nearest-training-point distances.
- **Predictions and their outcomes:** `docs/predictions-and-outcomes.md`.
